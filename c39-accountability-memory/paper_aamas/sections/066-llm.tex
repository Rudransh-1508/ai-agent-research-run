\subsection{Artifacts a model actually wrote}
\label{app:llm}

Both experiments above generate artifacts from a distribution we chose. This one
does not. Three agents review a real code change --- a connection-pool size
reduction shipped alongside an unrelated fix --- each taking a distinct aspect.
With $S_i = 1$ the agent is given the diff and must cite identifiers or line
numbers; with $S_i = 0$ it is told to produce confident, professional review
guidance \emph{without} the code and not to admit it. That is shirking in the
sense this paper means: the artifact still looks like work. A coordinator reads
the three reviews and ships or holds. The records are derived from the episode
exactly as in Section~\ref{app:episodes}, a judge model predicts which agents
actually read the diff, and the paper's instruments are applied to the induced
channel. Model \texttt{codestral-2508} at temperature $0$; $192$ episodes, $24$
per effort profile; all artifacts, records and verdicts are in the artifact.

\paragraph{Thirty bytes of authorship.}
The full trace and the artifact multiset hold the same five reviews. They differ
by about $30$ bytes of attribution labels, a $1.2\%$ difference in size, and by
$0.943$ bits of attribution error --- $95\%$ bootstrap interval
$[0.802, 1.089]$ over $20{,}000$ resamples, excluding zero. Roughly a third of
the recoverable per-agent signal lives in the labels rather than in the content,
on text a language model wrote. This is the identity-versus-volume finding of
Section~\ref{app:episodes} reproduced outside the generative model that produced
it, and it is the claim this experiment is offered to support.

\paragraph{What the gap is made of.}
A shuffled multiset invites an obvious objection: of course authorship cannot be
recovered once it is removed. The decomposition answers it. Splitting the error
into what the record says about \emph{how many} agents worked and what it says
about \emph{which}, the multiset recovers the count about as well as the full
trace does---mean count error $0.438$ against $0.391$---while its assignment
error accounts for essentially all of the $0.943$-bit gap. Taking the best of the
$3!$ relabellings of the judge's answer reduces the multiset's error from $1.333$
to $0.438$, exactly its count error.

This matters because re-identification was possible in principle and did not
happen. Each agent answers a \emph{different} sub-question, so the reviews are
topically self-identifying: a passage about connection pooling could only be the
second reviewer's. The zero-shot judge nonetheless did not reconstruct the
assignment from content.

We are careful about how far that generalises. Dietrich~\cite{dietrich2026peers}
shows that a \emph{trained} stylometric classifier recovers which model wrote an
anonymised text with macro $F_1$ near $0.99$, so authorship signal certainly
survives anonymisation and a dedicated re-identifier could plausibly recover much
of what our judge missed. Our claim is correspondingly narrow: under the
compaction a deployed system would actually apply, and read by the kind of
general-purpose judge such a system would actually use, the assignment is not
recovered. Whether an adversary or auditor equipped with a fitted stylometric
model could recover it is a different question, and one our design does not
answer.

\paragraph{Where our predictions failed, and the exact reason.}
Four of the five predictions registered for this experiment were falsified, and
the cause is a property of our task rather than of the records. Per-agent
classification accuracy on the full trace is $0.990$ and $0.943$ for the first
two agents but $0.677$ for the third, whose sub-question asks what is now
untested---a question a content-free answer imitates well. Because $\gamma$ is a
\emph{minimum} over agents, that one agent sets the margin, and it does so only
for the records that preserve identity: for the full trace the binding agent's
margin is $0.075$ against $0.958$ and $0.792$ for the others, whereas for the
multiset all three agents sit between $0.207$ and $0.335$, with none detectable.

Restricting the minimum to the two comparable agents restores the registered
ordering exactly---$0.792 \ge 0.744 > 0.207 \ge 0.083 \ge 0$---which is
Prediction~L1 as written. We report that as a diagnosis, not as a rescue: the
ordering was registered over all three agents and it failed over all three
agents.

Two things follow. First, a measured $\gamma$ is only as informative as the
\emph{least auditable} sub-task in the episode, so sub-tasks must be matched for
auditability before any empirical $\gamma$ can be attributed to a record; that is
a constraint on measuring the quantity at all, and it applies to anyone
attempting it. Second, our design confounds sub-question with agent position,
because agent $i$ always received sub-question $i$. We therefore cannot say
whether the third agent is hard to audit because of its question or because of
where it sits in the record, and the data cannot separate them. Randomising the
assignment would; we did not, and we do not claim the distinction.

\paragraph{One honest artefact of sample size.}
At four episodes per profile the same estimator returned
$\gamma(\text{full trace}) = 0$, triggering the failure condition registered
before the run. The cause was seven cells in which the judge happened to give
identical verdicts with and without some agent's effort, forcing an exact zero;
such ties can only bias $\gamma$ downward. They fell to one cell at ten episodes
per profile and to none at twenty-four. The attribution gap, by contrast, moved
from $0.969$ to $0.943$ across that sixfold increase in data.
