\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In a contemporary multi-agent system, a coordinator that fans work out to several
sub-agents does not keep what they did. Each sub-agent runs in its own context
and returns a summary; the messy part stays behind. When the coordinator's own
context approaches its limit, it is compacted---older exchanges, including
whatever it retained about its sub-agents, are replaced by a summary of
themselves. The budget forcing this is a fixed context window, and unlike a
storage bill it cannot be paid off. Sampling, the standard answer to trace
volume, is not available either: one cannot discard $99.9\%$ of the episode that
is currently running.

What survives is then the only evidence there is. Any later question about the
episode---which sub-agent's work to trust, which to re-run, what to report, whom
to credit or pay---is answered from the compressed artifact, after the fact. The
same object must therefore do two jobs at once:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reconstruction.} Recover what the team did, accurately enough to
      act on.
\item \textbf{Accountability.} Retain enough about each agent's own contribution
      that a later, bounded payment still rewards effort---whatever the
      teammates did.
\end{enumerate}

These pull in opposite directions. Reconstruction spends a small label budget
well by \emph{merging} profiles that are cheap to confuse. Accountability
requires the opposite: each agent, acting alone, must visibly move the record.
This paper measures that collision exactly.

\paragraph{Settling from a merged record is already a live failure.}
Not at a size bound---we know of no deployed system that both pays money and
compresses across agents under one---but the \emph{accountability} half of the
problem is already paid for. In Bittensor~\cite{bittensorweightcopier},
validators score miners by submitting weight vectors; those vectors are merged by
consensus, and validator dividends are keyed to agreement with the merged result.
A validator that evaluates nothing and submits the stake-weighted average of
everyone else's vector is, in the record, indistinguishable from one that did the
work---and reportedly does better: optimised weight copiers earn higher dividends
per stake than honest validators~\cite{bittensorfreeriding}. The deployed remedy
was not more monitoring but a change to the \emph{record}: weights are encrypted
until stale, so copies fall away from consensus~\cite{bittensorcommitreveal}.
We cite this as evidence that settling from a merged record destroys unilateral
sensitivity in practice, and as nothing more. Its binding constraint is temporal
and cryptoeconomic, not a bound on record size, so it is not an instance of the
frontier computed here; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} says so again.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Payment-decoder elimination} (Theorem~\ref{thm:elim}). A minimax
      argument removes the payment rule from the problem, leaving a condition on
      the record alone: \emph{every unilateral deviation must move the retained
      label distribution by at least $\gamma$ in total variation.} This is the
      general statement of the phenomenon, and it turns a bilinear search into a
      feasibility question over $2^n$ binary variables.
\item \textbf{The exact one-bit frontier} (Theorem~\ref{thm:onebit}):
      $\Dopt(2,\gamma) = \Mn + n\gamma(\frac{n-1}{2} - \Mn)$ for
      $0 \le \gamma \le 1/n$, linear in $\gamma$, for arbitrary bounded payments
      and arbitrary reconstruction.
\item \textbf{The exact flatness threshold} (Theorem~\ref{thm:kflat}):
      $\Kflat(n) = \gtwo(\Qn)$, with an explicit optimal record attaining margin
      $1 - 1/\dmin \ge 1/2$. The reduction is the contribution; the invariant is
      classical and we claim no novelty for it or for its values. Evaluating it
      gives $\Kflat(3)=4$, $\Kflat(5)=10$, $\Kflat(7)=32$ and
      $97 \le \Kflat(9) \le 128$, each with a certificate. The bracket at
      $n = 9$ is a gap in what is known about $\gtwo$, not a gap in the
      theorem: the identity is exact at every $n$, and only the invariant's
      value is open there.
\item \textbf{The uniform prior is load-bearing} (Theorem~\ref{thm:prior},
      Corollary~\ref{cor:tilt}). The threshold is characterised for every
      full-support prior. For any \emph{tilted} product prior it equals
      $2^n - (n-1)$, whatever the tilt: the free compression ratio collapses
      from $\Theta(n)$ to $1 + O(n2^{-n})$. The symmetric case is the most
      favourable one for accountability, not a neutral choice.
\item \textbf{The engineering reading} (Section~\ref{sec:agents}). The free
      compression ratio is $\Theta(n)$ and at most $(n+2)/2$: a record escapes
      the tension only by retaining about $85\%$ of per-agent resolution at
      $n = 20$. One retained bit per agent is always safe; an aggregate such as
      ``how many agents succeeded'' is far inside the tension regime.
\item \textbf{Mechanisms, measured} (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}). We evaluate
      compaction mechanisms nobody designed to be optimal---counts, thresholds,
      hashes, partial per-agent retention---by computing their exact margin
      through Theorem~\ref{thm:elim}. Records exist that are
      reconstruction-optimal at their budget and admit \emph{no} positive margin
      at any price; an aggregate count has margin exactly $1/n$; and retaining a
      strict subset of agents' bits is worth exactly nothing. On reviews
      written by a language model, two records holding the \emph{same} text
      and differing by $30$ bytes of authorship labels differ by $0.943$ bits
      of attribution error, $95\%$ CI $[0.802, 1.089]$ over $192$ episodes.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Every numerical claim is re-derived by an independent implementation that shares
no code with the one that produced it: $145$ checks, no solver and no search,
plus an audit that compares each number printed here against the artifact it came
from. The predictions in Section~\ref{sec:experiment} were registered and hashed
before the harness was written; two were wrong and are reported as wrong.

\paragraph{Roadmap: four moves.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:elim} removes the payment rule.} What is left is
      a condition on the record alone, so everything after it is a question about
      compression rather than about mechanisms.
\item \textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:onebit} gives the exact tradeoff under extreme
      compression} --- one surviving bit --- for arbitrary bounded payments and
      arbitrary reconstruction.
\item \textbf{Theorem~\ref{thm:kflat} gives the point at which the tradeoff
      disappears}, and identifies it with a classical graph invariant.
      Theorem~\ref{thm:prior} then shows how much of that depends on symmetry.
\item \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:experiment} shows real compaction mechanisms
      behave as predicted}, and that the thing they lose is agent identity.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:agents} converts the thresholds into bits per agent,
Section~\ref{sec:design} states the design rules that follow,
Section~\ref{sec:related} places the result, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what the model does not cover. Proofs
omitted for space, and the reproducibility package, are in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
