\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We state these plainly; each bounds a claim made above.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Uniform prior and binary effort.} Every threshold result in this
      paper is a uniform-prior result. Theorem~\ref{thm:kflat} is stated in the
      dominating regime, where the uniform prior admits a dominating optimal
      code; for a general prior we have only the necessary condition of
      Proposition~\ref{prop:necessity-general}, and the threshold is open
      (Section~\ref{sec:priors}, which also records a claim from an earlier
      version of this work that we withdraw). The uniform prior is equally
      load-bearing for
      Theorem~\ref{thm:onebit}: the orientation-alignment step uses
      flip-invariance of the uniform prior, and a non-uniform prior breaks that
      technique rather than shifting a constant. The one-bit frontier under a
      general prior is open. Effort is binary and the reconstruction loss is
      symmetric Hamming; weighted losses and graded effort are open.

\item \textbf{One value is open.} $\Kflat(9) \in [97,128]$, and
      $\gtwo(\Qn)$ has no closed form---it is a known-hard invariant. We regard
      this as a property of the reduction rather than a defect of it:
      Theorem~\ref{thm:kflat} says the threshold \emph{is} that invariant, so its
      hardness is inherited, not introduced.

\item \textbf{One-shot.} There is no repeated interaction, reputation, external
      audit channel, or shared randomness outside the counted record. Each of
      these can restore incentives and each changes the question; Remark
      \ref{rem:counting} explains why they must be excluded for the tradeoff to
      have content.

\item \textbf{Measuring $\gamma$ empirically needs matched sub-tasks, and our
      design does not provide them.} Because $\gamma$ is a minimum over agents,
      the least auditable sub-task in an episode sets it. In
      Section~\ref{sec:llm} one of three review questions is far harder to audit
      than the others---per-agent accuracy $0.677$ against $0.990$ and
      $0.943$---and it binds the margin for the two identity-preserving records,
      which is why four registered predictions failed there. Restricting the
      minimum to the two comparable agents reproduces the registered ordering
      exactly, but the prediction was made over all three agents and failed over
      all three. Separately, agent $i$ always receives sub-question $i$, so
      sub-question difficulty and position in the record are confounded and this
      data cannot separate them. Both are fixable---match sub-tasks for
      auditability, randomise the assignment---and neither is fixed here. We
      therefore report attribution error, which averages over agents, and treat
      the measured $\gamma$ ordering as uninformative about record type.

\item \textbf{The experiment is exact, and synthetic.}
      Section~\ref{sec:experiment} enumerates every profile, so its numbers carry
      no sampling error---but the mechanisms are stylised compaction rules on a
      binary effort model, not instrumented runs of a deployed agent stack. It
      establishes that records which are reconstruction-optimal and
      accountability-dead exist and are easy to build by accident; it does not
      establish that any particular production system has built one. Measuring
      per-agent bit retention in a real compaction step is the obvious next step
      and we have not taken it.

\item \textbf{The empirical case is assembled from two halves.} We are not aware
      of a single deployed system that both pays money and compresses across
      agents under a storage bound within an episode. The systems that aggregate
      across agents under a hard budget (context compaction) use the result for
      orchestration and reporting; the system that settles money from a merged
      record (Bittensor) is bounded temporally rather than in size. We claim the
      tension for deferred accountability, and we do not claim that conjunction
      has been observed.

\item \textbf{Frameworks that aggregate today could stop cheaply.} Where
      production agent frameworks lose per-agent cost attribution, they do so by
      default convenience and not under a binding bound; the attribution is
      recoverable with modest instrumentation. That is a weaker premise than a
      genuinely bounded record, and we do not lean on it.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Open directions.}
Closing $\Kflat(9)$---plausibly via published bounds on $\mu$-fold
$1$-coverings of length $9$; non-uniform and weighted settings; and graded rather
than binary effort.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Five, stated together so they are read together. \emph{Binary effort:} each agent
contributes one bit, and graded or continuous effort is not covered; the
reduction to $\gtwo$ is a statement about the cube specifically.
\emph{Synthetic episodes:} Section~\ref{sec:episodes} generates artifacts from a
two-distribution model we chose, so its numbers characterise that model, not any
deployed stack---what transfers is the ordering of record types, not the
magnitudes. \emph{One-shot:} there is no repetition, reputation or future play,
each of which can restore incentives on its own. \emph{Bounded payments:} the
decoder is confined to $[0,1]^K$; unbounded or state-dependent transfers are a
different problem. \emph{No external channel:} no audit, no shared randomness, no
side information outside the counted record---Remark~\ref{rem:counting} explains
why admitting any of them makes the tradeoff vacuous rather than merely weaker.
Each of these makes the model a lower bound on what a real system faces, except
the first, which we do not know how to sign.
