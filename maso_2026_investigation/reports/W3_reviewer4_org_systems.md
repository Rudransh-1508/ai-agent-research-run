## 1. Summary of the paper as I understand it

The authors build a fault-injectable multi-agent testbed on LangGraph around two workflows — a customer-service refund process adapted from tau2-bench retail, and a newly authored IT incident-management process — in which a single "organic" semantic fault (hallucinated tool arguments, corrupted handoffs, instruction conflicts, verification omission, agent death, memory poisoning, unauthorized action) is injected with known ground truth. The independent variable is organizational structure: flat vs. two-level vs. three-level delegation hierarchy, with/without monitor ("sentinel") agents, with/without resource scarcity. The principal measurement contribution is the Organizational Blast Radius (OBR): the fraction of seven typed "organizational boundaries" (role, communication, authority/delegation, tool/resource, memory, accountability, human-agent) crossed by provenance-traced corrupted events. Six containment/recovery policies (none, retry, verifier gate, checkpoint-rollback, sentinel quarantine + re-delegation, scripted-oracle human escalation with priced oversight minutes) are compared on a cost–utility Pareto frontier over ~3,400 runs. The paper then tests pre-registered hypotheses derived from classical MAS fault-handling — Dellarocas & Klein's "unzippering" propagation (H1), Hägg-style sentinel containment (H2), containment-backfires-under-scarcity (H3), and the predicted breakdown of classical mechanisms under semantic faults (H4) — using TOST equivalence testing so that "classical prediction fails" is a designed-in outcome.

The engineering is serious, the statistics are unusually honest for this genre, and the venue fit is real. My problem is more fundamental: **this is a network-propagation study wearing organizational vocabulary, and the vocabulary is doing work the constructs cannot support.**

## 2. Strengths (grudgingly)

- The experimental hygiene is well above the LLM-MAS field's baseline: deterministic DB-state ground truth as primary endpoint, fault-trigger verification, within-instance pairing, pre-registered hypotheses with equivalence bounds, and an explicit statement of which effect sizes the design cannot detect. I wish more submissions did this.
- Treating structure as an independent variable rather than a fixed scaffold is the right instinct, and varying it under *injected, non-adversarial* faults rather than adversarial attacks is a genuine gap (the adversarial corner is crowded; the organic-fault corner is not).
- The recovery-cost vector — tokens, tool calls, wall-clock, and priced human-oversight minutes — is the right *shape* of instrument for a cost–utility Pareto, even if (below) the human component is not measuring what it claims.
- The classical-MAS lineage engagement (Dellarocas & Klein ICMAS 2000, DOI 10.1109/ICMAS.2000.858440; Hägg 1997, DOI 10.1007/BFb0030090; Horling & Lesser 2004, DOI 10.1017/S0269888905000317) is more than this community usually manages, and H4 (where classical predictions break under semantic faults) is the one hypothesis with real suspense.

## 3. Weaknesses — the rejection case, ranked by severity

### W1 (fatal). The "organizations" are agent pipelines with role labels; no organizational construct is actually operationalized

Strip the vocabulary and the independent variable is: DAG depth ∈ {1, 2, 3} over a fixed roster of five roles, where "authority" is a spending-limit tool gate, "memory" is a shared scratchpad key, "accountability" is a provenance tag in a log, and "human escalation" is a scripted function call. None of the constructs that make something an *organization* rather than a *workflow* are present:

- **Decision rights and real authority.** Organization economics distinguishes *formal* authority (who is nominally entitled to decide) from *real* authority (who effectively decides, given who holds the information) — Aghion & Tirole, "Formal and Real Authority in Organizations," JPE 1997, DOI 10.1086/262063. This distinction is not a nicety; it is arguably *the* question for LLM agent orgs, because an LLM "supervisor" holds formal authority while the frontline agent holds all the situational information — the supervisor can only "opine," not verify (cf. the Loop-Back Authority result, arXiv:2609.14767, that a supervisor tier pays for itself exactly when it can verify). The paper's authority boundary is a permission bit on a tool. That is access control, not authority.
- **Accountability.** In the organizational literature, accountability is a *relation*: an actor obliged to explain and justify conduct to a forum that can pose questions, pass judgment, and impose consequences (Bovens, "Analysing and Assessing Accountability: A Conceptual Framework," European Law Journal 2007, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0386.2007.00378.x). Nothing in this testbed answers to a forum; nothing is sanctioned; no account is given. A provenance trace is *traceability* — a precondition for accountability, not the construct. Labeling a log edge an "accountability boundary crossing" is a category error.
- **Organizational memory.** The organizational-memory construct (Walsh & Ungson, AMR 1991, DOI 10.2307/258607) spans individuals, culture, transformations, structures, ecology, and archives — and its organizational significance is precisely that memory is distributed, political, and decays unevenly. Here "memory" is one LangGraph state key, relegated to a 360-run sub-study that the authors' own descope ladder cuts first under schedule pressure.
- **Routines.** The organizational-routines literature (Nelson & Winter 1982; Feldman & Pentland, ASQ 2003, DOI 10.2307/3556620) — the closest thing org science has to a theory of how organizations absorb or amplify local deviations — is entirely absent. The policy.md/SOP is static scenery, not a routine with ostensive and performative aspects that drift under fault conditions.
- **Span of control, norms, stakes.** Span of control is fixed by the fixed roster (and is an open, unstudied gap in this literature — a missed opportunity rather than a crime). More deeply: organizational containment mechanisms work through agents with *stakes* — reputation, career, blame-avoidance, trust repair. LLM agents have none. The causal mechanisms by which real organizations contain faults do not exist in this substrate; what exists is graph shape.

The MASO CFP's own topic list names decision rights, accountability, and organizational memory as distinct areas. A paper that puts "Organizational" in its title while operationalizing none of them is making a claim its instrumentation cannot cash.

### W2 (fatal). OBR is a graph-reachability count with organizational labels, not an organizational construct

OBR = |boundaries crossed by corrupted events| / |boundaries|. Three construct-validity failures:

1. **The typology comes from nowhere.** Seven boundary types are asserted, not derived — not from MOISE+/AGR/OperA (the computational-org models this venue's community actually built: structural/functional/deontic dimensions, roles-groups-links), not from organization theory, not from the dependability taxonomy (Avizienis et al., IEEE TDSC 2004, DOI 10.1109/TDSC.2004.2) whose fault→error→failure chain is the metric's actual intellectual parent. If the boundary set is ad hoc, the fraction is uninterpretable: add an eighth boundary type and every OBR value in the paper moves.
2. **Crossings are not commensurable.** A corrupted handoff message (communication) and an unauthorized $10k refund (authority) each count as one boundary crossing. Organizationally these differ by orders of magnitude in consequence, detectability, and remediability. Equal-weight counting over a nominal typology is not measurement; it is enumeration. No weighting scheme, no severity model, no sensitivity analysis over the typology is described.
3. **No validation of any kind.** No content-validity argument (why these seven?), no convergent/discriminant analysis (does OBR correlate with anything an organization would recognize as damage — utility loss is reported *separately*, which begs the question of what OBR adds), no demonstration that OBR behaves differently from a plain count of corrupted downstream nodes. If you renamed OBR "fraction of typed edges crossed," nothing in the paper would change. That is the tell: the organizational content is in the nomenclature, not the construct.

### W3 (severe). The human-escalation arm assumes away forty years of human-factors evidence — in the direction that flatters the mechanism

The "human" in the human-agent boundary condition is a scripted oracle: perfect policy knowledge, fixed latency, bounded capacity, sees only what the escalator surfaces. The human-factors literature has documented, for four decades, that real humans supervising automated systems fail in exactly the ways that matter for containment: the ironies of automation (Bainbridge, Automatica 1983, DOI 10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 — the operator degraded by automation is asked to rescue it); the out-of-the-loop performance problem (Endsley & Kiris, Human Factors 1995, DOI 10.1518/001872095779064555); automation misuse/disuse (Parasuraman & Riley, Human Factors 1997, DOI 10.1518/001872097778543886). The current agent-oversight literature has now shown these failures empirically *in this exact setting*: realized safety is an inverted-U in escalation rate because oversight is fatiguing and floodable (Oversight Has a Capacity, arXiv:2606.08919), and human confirmation gates are silently bypassable by low-salience faults (Invisible Ink Threats, arXiv:2608.02018) — i.e., precisely the subtle semantic faults this paper injects.

So the C5 arm does not measure "human escalation"; it measures an infallible, tireless escalation *function*. The paper's headline crossover — the price at which human oversight beats automated containment — is an artifact of an oracle human, and the bias direction is known: it inflates C5. The authors' own risk register (per the feasibility planning this design rests on) flags "oracle human too strong" as a reviewer-rejection risk; a noisy-oracle sensitivity over error rates {1.0, 0.8, 0.6} is contemplated but a 0.6–0.8-correct human is still not a human — it is a noisy function with no vigilance decrement, no workload, no attention economy, and no organizational position. If the human arm cannot be made behaviorally serious, it should be cut, not priced.

### W4 (severe). The "classical transfer tests" commit construct slippage: contract-net subcontracting is not line hierarchy

H1 claims to test Dellarocas & Klein's unzippering result. But D&K's substrate is the **Contract Net protocol**: market-style subcontracting trees where a contractor's death strands subcontracts that must be re-announced and re-bid. The paper's substrate is an **authority hierarchy** (frontline → supervisor → approver with verify-and-approve power). The markets-versus-hierarchies distinction is the founding distinction of organizational economics (Coase, "The Nature of the Firm," Economica 1937, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x): delegation across market boundaries and delegation down a line hierarchy are different governance forms with different failure physics — re-contracting cost versus blocked approval chains. Mapping D&K's depth-4 contracting tree onto a 3-level approval chain and calling the result a "replication" or "falsification" of unzippering is not a transfer test; it is an analogy with p-values. Either the claim must shrink to "a phenomenon reminiscent of unzippering occurs in LLM approval chains," or the testbed must actually implement contracting (re-announcement, re-bidding, subcontractor pools) — which the current design explicitly does not.

Relatedly, the "classical organizational fault-handling" corpus the paper engages is entirely the *agent-systems* lineage. The actual organizational-theory lineage on structure × failure is absent from both the hypotheses and the differentiation obligations: Carley & Lin's computational organization theory directly studied organizational performance under information distortion as a function of structure (Management Science 1997, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.43.7.976), and Lin & Carley's "Maydays and Murphies" program studied org design for *stress resistance* — the paper's exact question, asked of human organizations thirty years ago. Galbraith's information-processing theory (Interfaces 1974, DOI 10.1287/inte.4.3.28) *predicts* the paper's core mechanism — hierarchy exists as an exception-referral channel under uncertainty — and is nowhere cited. A reviewer from the organizational side of this workshop's audience will notice that the paper's "organizational" canon contains no organization science.

### W5 (severe). Single-fault injection is a distributed-systems epistemology, not an organizational-accident epistemology

The paper injects one fault, with known ground truth, and traces its provenance descendants. Organizational accident science has held for decades that this is not how organizations fail: accidents are alignments of *latent* failures through layered defenses (Reason, "Human error: models and management," BMJ 2000, DOI 10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768); they emerge from *normalized deviance* accumulated over time (Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision, Univ. of Chicago Press 1996, DOI 10.7208/chicago/9780226346960.001.0001); and they are catastrophic specifically under *interactive complexity* with *tight coupling* (Perrow, Normal Accidents, 1984; Princeton ed. DOI 10.1515/9781400828494). The two workflows studied — a linear refund pipeline and a tiered incident queue — are loosely coupled, interactively simple, and single-faulted: Perrow's least interesting quadrant. The high-reliability-organization literature (Weick & Sutcliffe, Managing the Unexpected, 2001/2015, DOI 10.1002/9781119175834) offers constructs that would map beautifully onto this testbed — preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, deference to expertise (route the fault to the most knowledgeable agent, not the highest one) — and the paper's mechanism menu (retry, gate, rollback, quarantine, escalate) contains none of them. What the paper measures is propagation physics on a labeled graph. That is a fine thing to measure; it is not organizational fault handling.

### W6 (moderate). Nothing here travels to the organizations in the room

MASO's audience includes industry and public-sector practitioners, and its CFP lists socio-technical transformation and deployment lessons as topics. What does a deploying organization take home? "Add a verifier gate; checkpoint-rollback dominates retry; sentinels backfire under scarcity." But scarcity here is a rate-limited approval tool, not headcount, attention, or budget politics; the human-oversight price λ_h is published but arbitrary, and the true costs organizations bear from propagated faults — customer trust, regulatory exposure, liability allocation (CFP topic 6), coordination rework — are not in the cost vector. The IT-incident workflow is author-invented when a mature, public, practitioner-recognized role structure exists (ITIL incident/problem management: service desk, tiered support, change advisory board); grounding W2 in ITIL would have cost little and bought face validity with exactly the practitioners the workshop courts. As it stands, the design recommendations do not survive contact with a real org chart.

### W7 (minor, but will be asked). Structural confounds in the IV

With a fixed five-role roster, varying flat/2-level/3-level confounds hierarchy *depth* with the *presence of verification authority*: the flat arm has no verifier at all, so "hierarchy contains faults better" may reduce to "someone checks." The design needs a flat-with-peer-verification control (which the classical literature — Kaminka & Tambe's socially-attentive monitoring, JAIR 2000, DOI 10.1613/jair.682 — would suggest) before depth effects are attributable to depth.

## 4. Questions to authors

1. Derive the seven boundary types: from which organizational model, and what happens to every OBR estimate if a reviewer proposes an eighth (e.g., norms, or informal communication)? Is there any sensitivity analysis over the typology?
2. What is the construct-validity argument for OBR — specifically, what does OBR predict that a plain count of corrupted downstream nodes does not, and why are boundary crossings equally weighted?
3. In the C5 arm, what is the justification that a scripted oracle with perfect policy knowledge and fixed latency measures anything about *human* escalation, given the inverted-U and silent-bypass results in the current oversight literature? Would your headline cost crossover survive a fatiguing, fallible oversight model?
4. Dellarocas & Klein's trees are Contract-Net subcontracting markets; your chains are authority hierarchies. Why is H1 a test of the same phenomenon rather than an analogy? What would falsify the claim that the mapping is valid?
5. Where, in your framework, are decision rights (as distinct from tool permissions), accountability to a forum (as distinct from provenance), and routines? If they are out of scope, what justifies "Organizational" in the title?
6. The flat arm has no verification authority; the hierarchical arms do. How do you separate the effect of depth from the effect of being checked?

## 5. Score

**Reject.** Confidence: **4/5** (high within my expertise; I flag that a classical-MAS reviewer may weigh the same design more kindly, and the statistical plan deserves acknowledgment regardless).

The engineering and inferential discipline are real, and H4 is a good question. But the paper's central noun is unsupported: the organizations are pipelines with role labels, the metric is graph reachability with org nomenclature, the human is a function, and the "classical organizational results" being tested are market-contracting results mapped onto authority hierarchies without engaging a single work of organization science. For a workshop whose CFP names decision rights, accountability, and organizational memory as first-class topics, that is disqualifying in this form.

## 6. The single change that would most improve my score

**Re-found the constructs on an explicit organizational model.** Specify the testbed organizations in a real organizational modeling framework (MOISE+/OperA-style: structural, functional, and deontic dimensions — roles, links, missions, *permissions and obligations*), derive the boundary typology from it, add at least one genuine decision-rights manipulation (e.g., formal vs. real authority: vary whether the supervisor can *verify* or only *opine*, à la Aghion & Tirole), and replace the oracle human with a behaviorally-parameterized oversight model (fatigue, automation bias, vigilance decrement — parameters from the HF literature, with sensitivity analysis). With that single re-founding, OBR becomes a construct instead of a count, H1–H3 become tests instead of analogies, and the paper earns its title. Without it, the honest title is "Fault Propagation and Containment in Structured LLM Agent Pipelines" — a decent paper for a different venue, and not this one.
